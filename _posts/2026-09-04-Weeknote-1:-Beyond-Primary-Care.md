## Farewell to Primary Care

This was my first real week in Referrals and Appointments. It took me a while to move as I had to hand over my previous role in Digital Primary Care ("DPC").

In my two-and-a-half years in Primary Care I wasn't managing your typical digital product or portfolio. Instead I owned the mechanism we use to implement change (from our national centre) into external, privately owned General Practice IT suppliers, also known as Electronic Patient Record ("EPR") Systems. The main focus of this role was working cross-organisationally to "shape-up" changes to be published onto the GPIT roadmap, from flagship national propositions like Vaccinations, Proxy Access and HealthCheck Online, to smaller urgent changes that may affect patient safety or clinical risk.

Although the role was very challenging, for a plethora of reasons, it was also hugely rewarding. It was a great way to start my journey into two new areas, the public sector and healthcare, and it gave me a deep understanding and experience of the systemic challenges the NHS has around delivering innovative products and services. I will write more about GPIT in depth in another post.

## The new role

I was ready to take that learning and apply it to a new area, one that had real national-scale impact, and a product built "for the NHS by the NHS", aligned with the value extolled by the Digital Prevention Services Portfolio. The Electronic Referrals Service is a great example of where we, as a national digital centre, have a strong right to play and can build something that helps to join up services, improve patient pathways and reduce clinical burden.

It exists closely with other key national platforms like Patient Care Aggregator (aka Wayfinder) and the Booking and Referrals Standard (aka BaRS), which helps join up hospital EPR systems and allow patients to see their appointments in the NHS App.

As expected, there's a lot going on in Referrals and Appointments, and with a steep learning curve typical of working in NHS England I now have a lot of reading up, intro meetings and work to do to get up to speed.

## Setting my intention

Starting somewhere new gives you a good reason to take a step back and think about what's most important from a product perspective. It's a good opportunity to grab, especially when you have some headspace before getting pulled into the inevitable daily problem solving that always tries to take over your role. It allows you to be considered about the priority and sequencing of getting up to speed.

This means starting by building an understanding of the basics. It's good to acknowledge that this will also be an ongoing endeavour:

- **Outcomes** - understanding the overarching "why/s". Why does this exist, what are the benefits? What is our strategy?
- **Products** - what are the products and services? What user needs do they address?
- **Users** - who are our users? What are their jobs-to-be-done and main problems to solve?
- **System** - what is the wider context that these products fit within? The market? Why do these problems exist?
- **Technology** - what is the technical landscape, current architecture and ecosystem, and how does it fit together?
- **Stakeholders** - who are we working with? What are their priorities?

In this new role, and learning from my experience in GPIT, I intend to be more considered about how I apply my effort in Referrals and Appointments: committing my effort to things that are most likely to deliver value, and finding the right balance between day-to-day management and keeping some headspace to remain strategic and think ahead. My biggest value to the organisation is to lead teams to deliver the right outcomes, rather than just absorbing BAU work.

To do this, I also put together a bit of a framework with a high-level 90-day plan to help give me focus and manage expectations, and shared it with my Head of Product to discuss. I will start by listening intently to people, pick up signals and take it from there.

## Initial observation: Working with suppliers

An interesting trait of being a permanent NHSE staff member working at Product Lead 8c level and above is that you often have a lot of supplier management duties along with the usual product responsibilities, e.g. managing internal staff but also large teams of contract staff.

Generally, there are always loads of challenges around staffing. We are continuously at the end of the last restructure and the start or middle of the latest one (from what I have experienced), and most often the programme has a big reliance on supplier contracts to deliver. This enables you to be responsive and scale up and down at speed if necessary.

This means working with teams from Accenture, CGI, PA Consulting, IBM, Hippo and Kainos, to name but a few. They each have their unique strengths and characteristics that you start to pick up on working here. Some act more as internal blended teams and some maintain more of a client/supplier structure. Some are more process driven and some are more sales driven. This should also be reflected in how you structure your contracts.

The big challenge is ensuring that contract and permanent staff are aligned around core outcomes and strategy, and focused on delivering solutions that address user needs. NHSE staff are by nature very invested in this. Supplier staff, however, also have to balance the goals of their business and have a secondary motivation, which is achieving the goals of their own organisation, usually more focused on revenue.

This is why we place emphasis on working as part of an effective "Rainbow Team". A central part of this is to acknowledge these slightly divergent motivations and land on a common goal.

However, it's clear there is currently a tension between the permanent staff and the contract staff, and I'm sure it will be a big part of my role to bring them together and coordinate around our shared outcomes. There is always a level of pragmatism that must be applied to delivering at NHS England; you would be naive to think otherwise.
