## Understanding the space and RTT

Referrals and Appointments covers a big area, from Primary to Elective care. Elective care is planned, non-urgent care: essentially, care that can be scheduled. This is the part of the NHS you hear about in the media, with talk of waiting lists and reasonable timeframes for receiving care.

It also touches on the flagship metric, referral-to-treatment ("RTT"), which measures the time from first referral through to receiving treatment. I'm hearing a lot about this, particularly where the RTT clock starts and stops. On the surface it sounds simple, but nothing here is simple...

Within RTT there's a complex web of sub-tasks, rules and services. For instance, if a referral is made and the service provider rejects it, should the clock stop? What about when the referrer is asking for advice rather than sending a formal referral? Should that start the clock? From the patient's perspective, they have started their interaction with the service and are waiting for treatment. There's a lot more to unpack here...

My experience here is fairly limited, but this is a big part of designing digital products and services in the public sector. A helpful analogy: Policy defines the rules of the game, and we do our best to deliver digital services that align with those rules as efficiently as possible, meet user needs, are clinically safe, and don't increase clinical or administrative burden. Ultimately, our services should give healthcare professionals more time to deliver care directly to patients.

However, if we're really effective and find enough compelling evidence and data to prove the rules of the game need to change, we can go up the line and influence Policy. This is really exciting stuff, and I'm sure we'll become more joined-up in future.

## Getting closer to the work

I'm getting closer to the work now, picking up actions to close out Q3 planning and identifying areas that need more attention:

- **Calling out the "why":** clearer, more accessible articulation of the why, including the specific measurable benefit of each roadmap item.
- **Calling out risks and assumptions:** in such a complex environment we must have clear sight of the deal-killer risks and manage them in advance. This is where we have a real duty to manage public investment.
- **A clearer thread to the overarching benefits:** we've committed to delivering big benefits, so every piece of work we invest in needs a clear line showing how it contributes to them.

This isn't straightforward. These product areas are technical and complex, with a number of very similar API calls that do similar-but-different things. For instance, should "referral redirect" work with a new referral number or the existing one? Separately, should an "onward referral" to a new service start a new RTT clock?

This all needs unpacking, but it's near impossible to steer the team without understanding the right level of detail, including the quantifiable value of each proposed roadmap item. This is my primary BAU focus: supporting the teams, optimising the backlog, and getting to know the product well enough to steer teams and face off to stakeholders.

## Coming back to the "why"

I'm working with the Central Benefits team, the Measure Our Impact team and the Transformation and Adoption team. A core part of a product role is making sure stakeholders are aligned around the outcomes. What are the user outcomes and the organisational outcomes? Against these we can speak openly about impact, risks and issues, and give our strong recommendation. What is our position, backed up by user research?

Because of the sheer scale of our national products, we're talking £billions of benefits, and that comes with a lot of pressure and scrutiny, rightly so.

You can judge the maturity of an area by how well teams can answer two questions:

1. Why are we doing this?
2. What is the value?

Product should act as an anchor, a weight constantly bringing us back to the why.

## Culture and creating the environment

Following Pete Ward's call-out about celebrating our successes, I've started contributing to team culture. We recently shipped a feature that raises the file attachment limit for clinicians from 5MB to 100MB. Until now, anyone sending a large file had to find workarounds, compressing it or chopping it into pieces under 5MB. Those workarounds are time-consuming, and at our scale they're costly to the system.

To celebrate, I created some options for file attachment stickers, some featuring Clippy, the most characterful paperclip in history (!), and got the team to vote on their favourite. It may feel a bit sentimental, but I think it adds a necessary lightness and fun to work, which is much needed in a tough environment.
