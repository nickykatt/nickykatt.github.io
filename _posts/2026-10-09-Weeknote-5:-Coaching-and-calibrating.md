Another busy week: more careful management of a controversial product feature, some opportunity sizing and benefits work, and a brilliant chat with a product coach who helped me recalibrate my focus towards organisational outcomes.

My secondary goal this week is to write a concise weeknote that I can finish off efficiently on Friday.

### Stick or twist?

Our portfolio has big ambitions, with forecasts stretching out to 2030. That's motivating from an impact perspective, but it also brings a lot of pressure and scrutiny from senior executives. We have weekly updates with some of the most senior execs, and their feedback tends to be very specific, detailed and often technical. This is challenging for the teams, who are in a weekly cycle of preparing carefully crafted exec updates (honest and constructive) alongside doing the actual work.

One feature that has already been committed to has uncovered a number of deal-killer technical and clinical assumptions during design. The feature was born out of new policy and a year of decisions balancing clinical risk, user value and technical complexity. Now the team must carefully manoeuvre it back into a viable position while mitigating those risks.

It's great to see the team pitch in under pressure and show how productive we can be as an agile, truly multidisciplinary unit.

### Opportunity sizing

I enjoy opportunity sizing: being entrepreneurial as a product manager, spotting opportunities and running quick "fag-packet" calculations to see which are worth pursuing. It feels creative, and it's motivating to see the potential impact.

Early in my career it finally clicked for me that, with some simple calculations and evidence-based research, most things can be quantified relatively easily, and doing so forces you to think about the human costs.

I want to encourage our PMs to be more creative and entrepreneurial in spotting these opportunities, quickly building an opportunity model, and submitting them as betas for the portfolio to commit to exploring.

In financial services we had good tools and support for this, including a book of base metrics: average salaries for different staff, total staff numbers, number of practices, and product data like referral volumes. In an ideal world we'd also have a robust, representative time-and-motion study of all our user activities to baseline against. Numbers and tools like these massively help when you're sketching up a new opportunity case.

### Product focus

This week I had my first proper session with a product coach, Roberta, and it was really useful. I'm always keen to discuss product with colleagues, but it was refreshing and hugely valuable to step back and talk, slightly more selfishly, about my own experiences and goals.

We've both worked across different-sized organisations outside the public sector, and we quickly identified something I've been wrestling with: how to recalibrate my focus and expectations after coming from fast-paced, results-driven organisations. I'm used to shipping products and getting them into users' hands, and that's my idea of success. Here we're playing a different game. You have to spend more time improving the conditions for teams and creating an environment where they can thrive.

We discussed putting more focus on internal outputs, such as processes, models and tools that improve our overall product capability. This is aligned with my objectives and is a big part of the <a href="https://understand-digital-data-roles-skills.service.gov.uk/role/product-manager/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Head of Product role as defined in the government's capability framework</a>, yet I often default to focusing on the product and hard benefits.

It was a good reminder, so I spent Thursday afternoon on our product governance workstream, particularly a strategy for a more assumption-driven product process. That includes the right level of governance through key SME reviews and proportionate stage-gate assurance. I usually have an immediate allergic reaction to heavy process, but in this environment it's necessary, and we have to find the right balance.

Again, this comes down to a reset in how we do product with our suppliers. Ultimately, it's about responsible management of public investment in services that have the best chance of delivering outcomes to the frontline.

It helps to think of the product lifecycle as a series of mini investment gates. Do we have enough confidence to keep investing in this opportunity? What cost-effective tests can we run, and what evidence can we gather, to validate our riskiest assumptions?
