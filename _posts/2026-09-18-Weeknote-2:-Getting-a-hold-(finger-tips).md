Starting to get a feel for the rhythm of the Referrals and Appointments programme, who the people to go to are, and what is most important to focus on. We are mid Q3 planning and I'm digging into the plans the teams are pulling together, to make sure there is a clear strategic thread back to what has been presented as SLT priorities.

Due to some delivery challenges over the last 6 months, there is some tension around delivering against the detail in the SOW. This is unfortunate because, ultimately, the stronger the trust between a supplier and the commissioning organisation, the less likely you are to need to revert to and scrutinise what is in the SOW.

We all know that plans change and that we can't confidently scope out our final deliverables in advance, so we should be able to contract effectively to support this. But when things go wrong, teams regress back to the detail outlined in the SOW. NHS England governance also requires a very well-defined scope and benefits to approve funding, which adds to this tension.

There must be a better way to contract around outcomes that are within the supplier's control, and around the successful completion of <a href="https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">product phases</a>: Discoveries, Alphas and Betas. Ideally there would be some incentive to reward suppliers for uncovering, in the most cost-effective way, that an idea is not feasible, viable or desirable, helping to save public money being over-invested in unviable solutions. <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-digital-data-and-technology-playbook/contracting-for-agile-guidance-note-html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">This guidance note</a> outlines an approach, and I will go on the hunt for where it has been successfully implemented.

## Stakeholder influence

I'm building my stakeholder map and understanding which stakeholders are most persuasive and who commands the room in meetings. I'm also getting a sense of who is *influential*, a subtle but important difference from being persuasive, as outlined in <a href="https://jennywanger.com/articles/influence-doesnt-come-with-the-promotion/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Jenny Wanger's blog</a> via the Mind the Product newsletter:

> "Persuasion is what happens in the room. You make the case, and the person in front of you either agrees or doesn't, right there, based on how well you argued it. It's a single event. Influence is what happens after you leave the room. It's whether the person you persuaded goes on to make your case to people when you're not there, because they've decided supporting you is the right call. Influence is the system that makes your next persuasion event easier to win."

I hope that by listening and engaging with my colleagues I'm able to develop influence.

## Assumption-based product delivery and pre-mortems

One of our Senior Leadership Team raised the term pre-mortem in a session last week. It was a new concept to me, and my initial reaction was that it felt too negative and wasn't something we'd want to present to our Policy colleagues. However, looking into it more, I understand it's about proactive risk management: creating a safe space for team members and wider stakeholders to identify what they believe are the big risks or assumptions to a product's success. Gary Klein's <a href="https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">original HBR piece</a> is a good starting point.

I'm a big driver of assumption-driven product development, especially in complex environments such as healthcare or financial services, where you have to traverse an incredibly complex, booby-trapped landscape to ship a successful product. It's incredibly valuable to identify, as early as possible, what the giant deal-killer assumptions are going to be. It's a skill I picked up during Innosight Bootcamp training at Experian, based on the theory in *Competing Against Luck* by Clayton M. Christensen.

## Imposter syndrome and picking battles

It's officially week 2, and although I'm starting to get involved in key meetings and deliver outputs, I'm still very much in the listen-and-understand phase. This can be difficult when you're used to being more productive, and I need to be self-aware, have self-compassion and be confident that I will, in good time, be back to my usual speed and level of output.

I love change. It invigorates me and I love getting stuck into new problem spaces. However, based on my previous experience of working in this organisation, I need to be more careful about where I apply my effort and which battles I pick.

## Calibrating expectations

I often speak to teams and individuals in other areas of product and platforms. I think this is essential to enriching my experience and helps to validate my approach in many ways.

I had lunch with Emily Houghton, who built the Personalised Prevention Services and Managing My Health teams. Emily has been a consistent guiding light in my journey at NHSE, creating an exciting, friendly and fun environment in the office as well as working at an incredible pace with passion and precision. I really admire her and value her input.

Over lunch we discussed the rollercoaster of starting a new role in NHSE when coming in from outside the public sector, and it rang true. It happened to me and I've now seen it happen to many others:

1. **You hit the ground running:** quickly identifying big and obvious problems that need to be solved.
2. **You start solving those problems:** at a surface level, but the further you go the deeper you get. The systemic problems have deep roots.
3. **You take on more:** underestimating the effort required to drive change. The systemic problems are so deep-rooted and complex, this place is insane!
4. **You push harder:** because you are resilient and experienced, you can handle a lot.
5. **It's too much and you crash:** the organisation can't change at pace, and driving any change means bringing a stakeholder army on the journey with you. This can happen in a month or in 2 years!

<img src="{{ '/assets/images/catastrophe_rollercoaster.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="Catastrophe rollercoaster" width="800">

This is dangerous territory: you start to question your own skills and abilities, and it's important to have some self-compassion. It's really important to remain a player in the game. You do not want to become a <a href="https://leadershiplaboratory.beehiiv.com/p/players-passengers-prisoners" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">prisoner or passenger</a>.
