A hectic week, with announcements on the new Department of Health structure, the first drafts of transformation papers being shared for review, and some Halloween surprises.

### NHS Group and delivery

This week there was an update from the Health Secretary, Yvette Cooper, on the abolition of NHS England and our merger with the Department of Health. I still feel somewhat of a spectator: it's a lot of change to absorb for such a complex organisation, and I'm still getting my head around what it means for us.

The headline was that as we merge into the DHSC we will remain in what is described as an NHS Group, and the vast majority of national delivery will sit there.

> "As we devolve power out to the frontline, the new DHSC must transform into a streamlined and agile organisation executing a core set of national functions effectively," Cooper said.
>
> "The national centre should offer local areas a single vision, clarity on the future strategy, and a set of national priorities and minimum standards. It should also oversee the execution of a sophisticated and clear rules-based system that rewards success, coordinates an overall improvement offer and intervenes where performance falls short."

<a href="https://www.civilserviceworld.com/professions/article/yvette-cooper-outlines-her-vision-for-dhsc-and-nhs-reform" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Yvette Cooper outlines her vision for DHSC and NHS reform</a> - Civil Service World

There continue to be mentions of performance management, incentives and reverting to the pre-2012 model of the NHS, before NHS England was established by the Lansley reforms. You can't disagree with the ambitions outlined, but an enormous amount of effort goes into changing shape, which takes a lot of energy from teams also delivering in-year targets and goals.

### Opportunity papers

As part of transformation, we have identified a number of focus areas and have been working up papers to baseline where we currently are in each of these areas and articulate a clear strategic approach.

Working for the NHS, we tend to focus on many of the same areas for a long period of time. This means we complete a lot of ongoing user research and have a lot of evidence about existing user needs and the problems to solve in key areas. So instead of starting from scratch, a lot of effort goes into gathering previous evidence to determine whether user needs have been validated and identifying where there may be gaps. When reviewing teams' work, it's important that we are confident any proposed solution addresses real, validated user needs and that users will adopt these new capabilities.

However, we are working at pace, so it pays to take a moment to step back and consider the best approach to creating the artefact. I've never been a strong advocate of there being a right and wrong way to do things; generally, with this type of work, it's about being focused on what we are trying to solve and prioritising efforts to get to that outcome. When preparing a paper, I think about:

- **Audience:** Who is the audience? What are their motivations? What do they respond well to?
- **Purpose:** What is the purpose of the paper?
- **Problem:** Is the problem clear and backed by data and evidence? What is the current state, and what are the constraints? What are the user needs, are they validated, and where are the gaps?
- **Opportunity:** What is the opportunity, and how much evidence have we gathered that it is worth pursuing? What are the outcomes, benefits and metrics?
- **Solution:** What is the proposed solution? What are the options and analysis?

I'm still building my domain knowledge, so it was a good exercise in going deeper into two specific areas that are crucial to this end-to-end service: sifting through the user research and needs repository, repositioning the focus areas to be more outcome-focused rather than solution-focused, and clearly outlining a recommendation based on analysis of several options.

It's also a good opportunity to collaborate with colleagues in the area, particularly Design and Technology, following a "Three Amigos" approach. Although I appreciate working remotely, I also miss face-to-face time and having a proper conversation. It's through conversations like this that humans really ideate at speed.

### Operating model and service ownership

As part of transformation, we're exploring how service ownership could work. There's lots to understand here, but from my perspective it makes sense to put more emphasis on service ownership: having a service owner who is dedicated to specific parts of the referrals and appointments service, represents users, owns strategy and outcomes, and works more closely with Policy. They can then operate in a more technology-agnostic way, enabling our capability teams to be more empowered in how they best meet those user needs and deliver outcomes.

However, more change means more overhead for people, and with all the announcements about new structures, I'm conscious that teams may feel cognitive overload trying to comprehend how this all fits together!

### The referral market

Alongside working up the product requirements documents and options papers this week, I spent some time researching the market of suppliers that deliver referral services on top of our APIs, in addition to our Professional Application UI. This is broadly because our UI and APIs, although they handle the majority of referrals every day, have gaps around things like better messaging, referral information, worklists and reporting, and this leaves gaps for the market to fill. Organisations have to pay to use these additional services on top of our "free" product offering.

This is a good example of what it's like to do product at NHS England. You have to really think about what our national role is and how what we do impacts the market. Should we continue to optimise our product to encourage a single, more affordable national referrals tool, or work with the market to accelerate innovation and not stand in its way?

We've been learning a lot from talking to suppliers in this space.
